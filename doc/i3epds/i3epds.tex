  \IEEEPARstart{I}{mage} processing demands have increased substantially in the last decade, due in part to the success of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in two-dimensional image recognition.everal accelerator architectures have been developed; a method that has seen wide success in 2D~\cite{mittal2020survey} and 3D~\cite{mittal2021survey} convolution.

  A variety of optimization approaches have been applied to the task of accelerating CNNs, such as: efficient memory access~\cite{}; massive parallelization methods~\cite{jouppi2020domain,choquette2021nvidia}; optimizing matrix-multiplication~\cite{} or the multiplication operation itself~\cite{fan2021high}; quantizing weights and activations~\cite{}; and deploying complete CNNs in FPGA technology~\cite{} often via software-integration systems such as HLS~\cite{}.

  One approach that has garnered attention in recent years is the application of more efficient convolution algorithms. The standard algorithm known as convolution-over-space, as well as the more widely used matrix-multiplication (MMX), have a base assymptotic complexity of $O(N^2)$, leading to an increasing demand for more efficient algorithms. This is particularly relevant to 3D CNNs, as the processing load of its convolution layers can quickly outscale every other layer in the network~\cite{hegde2018morph}.

  In 2016, Lavin \& Gray~\cite{lavin2016fast} reviewed the relevant convolution algorithms --- which were, at the time, MMX and Fourier convolution --- and proposed a new class of algorithms based on the Coppersmith–Winograd series of matrix multiplication algorithms~\cite{coppersmith1982asymptotic}. MMX Algorithms with lower assymptotic bounds have been discovered~\cite{}, however, they incur increasingly complex transforms which severely hinders applicability. As such, the so-called Winograd class of convolution algorithms has since become the standard for hardware-efficient CNN designs~\cite{shen2018towards,barabasz2019winograd,vardhana2025high}.

  Fourier convolution was deemed innefficient for modern CNN designs as its upsides only overweight its downsides for convolutions using large kernels~\cite{lavin2016fast}, a method which has lost prevalence to deeper networks that use many small (usually 3x3) kernels~\cite{simonyan2014very}. The algorithm is also not, as of yet, easily implemented in high-level designs, due in large part to its reliance on Complex-domain operations.
  
  The algorithm has since resurged in a range of applications better suited to apply it efficiently, such as spectral CNNs~\cite{pratt2017fcnn,liu2021accelerating}, including the implementation of generic neural-net functions in the spectral domain~\cite{ayat2019spectral,watanabe2021image}. New techquines were found to optimize key parts of the algorithm, including convolution splitting~\cite{chitsaz2020acceleration} complemented by overlap-add~\cite{chen2024fast}, and mixed-radix FFT~\cite{meng2024high}. And, as is the topic of this article, it has found success in 3D CNN applications~\cite{nguyen2016energy}\todo{cite at least one more}

  There remains, however, the issue of Complex-domain multiplication in non-spectral CNNs. Recent hardware implementations in FPGA have addressed the issue using parallelization~\cite{li2024split} and pipelining~\cite{fan2021high}, however, these methods do not increase the system's hardware-efficiency (measured in thoughput per component) as it they do not lower the number of operations required to complete Complex multiplications.
  
  We propose a polar-coordinate multiplication unit, designed along a pipelined FFT and IFFT chain, optimized with both input slicing and overlap-add. We synthesize the architecture using HDL and implement the it in a VC709 FPGA board. We demonstrate, via comparison with state-of-the-art benchmarks, the viability of Fourier convolution in Real-domain 3D CNNs.

  Fourier Convolution achieves lower complexity via the the Convolution Theorem. Let $a,b$ be two discrete signals in $\mathbb{R}^N$. The convolution of $a$ and $b$ can be represented as:
  %
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:theo}
    a * b = \mathcal{F}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}(a) \cdot \mathcal{F}(b)]\,,
  \end{equation}
  %
  where $\cdot$ represents the pointwise product and $\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathcal{F}^{-1}$ represent the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) and its inverse.

  The pointwise product of two signals incurs $O(N)$ multiplications, whereas convolution in the space domain incurs $O(N^2)$. There are two main caveats to this method: the signals must be transformed to-and-from the Complex domain via a DFT; and the pointwise operation must be performed in the Complex domain. We address these issues in Sections \ref{ssec:fft} and \ref{ssec:cmul}, respectivelly.

  We also make use of a decomposition algorithm to further decrease complexity, described in Section~\ref{ssec:alg}. The resulting system can be fully pipelined to increase its throughput, and the methods used for such as described in Section~\ref{ssec:pipe}.

  \subsection{FFT \& IFFT}\label{ssec:fft}
    FFT is a class of algorithms capable of computing an $N$-point DFT in $O(N.{\log}_{2}N)$. The radix-2 FFT~\cite{gauss1866nachlass,cooley1965algorithm} was chosen for this project due to being easily pipelined with few arithmetic components (see Section~\ref{ssec:pipe}).

    \begin{figure}[htb]\centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{fig/fft-0.pdf}
      \caption{Visual representation of an 8-point radix-2 DIT FFT.}\label{fig:fft}
    \end{figure}
    
    An $N$-point radix-2 FFT breaks down the DFT into ${\log}_{2}N$ steps, each consisting of $\frac{N}{2}$ ``butterfly operations'' followed by $N$ twiddle-factor multiplications; as illustrated on Figure~\ref{fig:fft}. Each ``butterfly'' receives a pair $(A,B)$ and outputs $(A+B,A-B)$, where $A$ and $B$ are Complex numbers. Each result is then multiplied by a corresponding root-of-unity --- known as \textit{twiddle-factor} --- with the form:
    %
    \begin{equation}
      \omega_{N}^{k} = e^{\frac{2{\pi}i}{N}k}\,,
    \end{equation}
    %
    where $k \in \mathbb{Z} \cap [0,N-1]$.

    While the input signal is one-dimensional, it is possible to represent an arbritrary number of dimensions using zero-padding. Let $M$ be a three-dimensional signal of size $M_x,M_y,M_z$. Figure~\ref{fig:Ma} represents $M$ given its dimensions all have size 2. To convolve this array with another signal of the same shape, we must first pad each dimension by $2.dim-1$, in order to convey its dimensionaliry and avoid cyclic convolution. Figure~\ref{fig:Mb} represents the padded signal; the green line representing the order of rasterization.

  \subsection{Complex Multiplication}\label{ssec:cmul}
    Naively, Complex-domain multiplication implies 4 Real-domain multiplications. Let $a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{R}$ and $X,Y \in \mathbb{C} \mid X=a+bi,Y=c+di$. As such:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cmul-rect}
      XY = (a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd) + (ad+bc)i\,.
    \end{equation}

    However, X and Y can also be represented in polar coordinates, or more formally $X,Y \in \mathbb{C} \mid X=A\phase{\alpha_1},Y=B\phase{\alpha_2}$, where $A,B$ are the radial distances and $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ are the respective angular coordinates. As such:
    %  
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cmul-pol}
      XY = A\phase{\alpha_1} \cdot B\phase{\alpha_2} = AB\phase{\alpha_1+\alpha_2}\,.
    \end{equation}

    This representation reduces the total operation count to 1 Real multiplication and 1 Real addition, which are mutually independent and can thus be parallelized.
    
    In order to convert to-and-from these representations, we implement the trigonometric algorithm CORDIC ({\bf{CO}}ordinate {\bf{R}}otation {\bf{DI}}gital {\bf{C}}omputer)~\cite{volder1959cordic}. This algorithm iterativelly calculates the rotation of a given vector by decreasingly large angles. It has two modes of operation: rotation and vectorization; as well as three inputs, here labeled $(x,y,z)$. In vectorization mode, the rectangular coordinates of a Complex number are inputed as $(x_0,y_0)$, and after $j$ iterations, $(x_j,z_j)$ are the radial distance and angular coordinate, respectively. Similarly, in rotation mode, the polar coordinates of a Complex number are inputed at $(x_0,z_0)$ and after $j$ iterations, $(x_j,y_j)$ are the respective rectangular coordinates. This is generally expressed as:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cordic1}
      \begin{bmatrix}
        x_{j+1} \\
        y_{j+1} \\
        z_{j+1}
      \end{bmatrix}
      =
      \begin{bmatrix}
        x_{j} - \sigma_{j}2^{-j}y_{j} \\
        y_{j} + \sigma_{j}2^{-j}x_{j} \\
        z_{j} + \sigma_{j}\arctan(2^{-j})
      \end{bmatrix}\,,
    \end{equation}
    %
    where
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cordic2}
      \sigma_j = 
      \begin{cases}
        sign(y_j);~in~\textbf{vectorization}~mode \\
        -sign(z_j);~in~\textbf{rotation}~mode
      \end{cases}\,.
    \end{equation}

    The precision of the algorithm is equivalent to $2^{-j}$, or 1-bit of precision per iteration. Note that when using fixed-point notation, it is enough to iterate once for each bit after the point. The total number of bits of a variable is herefore denoted $S_t$, and the number of bits after the point is denoted $S_p$. Our implementation defaults to 24 and 16, respectivelly.

    As such, a simple hardware implementation of CORDIC consists of two $S_t$-bit adders, one $S_p$-bit adder, two variable bit-shifters, three registers, and a lookup table for storing $\arctan(2^{-j})$.
    
    The outputs of $x_j$ and $y_j$ are scaled by $K_j$ on each iteration, where
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cordic3}
      K_j = \cos(\arctan(2^{-j}))\,,
    \end{equation}
    and must be corrected to obtain the desired result. The correction can be made after the process completes, by diving each output by the scaling factor:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cordic4}
      \frac{1}{K} = \prod_{j=0}^{S_p-1}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2^{-2j}}}} \stackrel{S_p=16}{\approx} 1.64676\,.
    \end{equation}

    In our implementation, CORDIC is used 3 times. The first two convert the input image slice and kernel to polar coordinates, and the last converts the result back to rectangular coordinates. The scaling factor carries over across operations, including pointwise multiplication, and as such we can apply the correction at any point in the process (image, kernel or product). The combined scaling factor is:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cordic5}
      \frac{1}{K^3} \stackrel{S_p=16}{\approx} 0.22393\,.
    \end{equation}

    The values of $\arctan(2^{-j})$ are also constant and can be pre-calculated and stored in a lookup table. However, the convolution operation requires adding two angular coordinates before the transformation back into rectangular coordinates. The resulting angle can exceed $\pi$ radians, causing issues with the algorithm. To circumvent this, we store the \textit{quadrant} of each angular coordinate in the unit circle, normalize them to the first quadrant, perform the add operation, and convert the result into the respective quadrant. To simplify this computation, we divide each $\arctan(2^{-j})$ by $2\pi$. The resulting binary, encoded in 16-bits, represents fractions of $\pi$, with its two most-significant bits representing the quadrant of the vector in the unit circle, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pfb}. This allows quick access to the quadrant of the angle, as well as enabling normalization to any quadrant by simply setting its first two bits.

    \begin{figure}[htb]\centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{fig/unit_circle-0.pdf}
      \caption{Four symmetric angles encoded as $\pi$-fractions.}\label{fig:pfb}
    \end{figure}
    
    CORDIC's complexity does not increase with image- or kernel-size, depending instead on $S_p$, giving it a relative asymptotic complexity of $O(1)$ and enabling a trade-off between numerical precision and operation complexity.

  \subsection{Algorithmic Decomposition}\label{ssec:alg}
    In order to convolve two inputs using FCONV, the size of the input arrays must be equivalent. However, in the field of image recognition, the image is often much larger than the convolution kernels. In order to avoid padding the kernel to match the image, we employ a decomposition algorithm known as Overlap-Add~\cite{smith1997scientist,chen2024fast}. The image is broken down into kernel-sized slices which are individually convolved with each kernel. Each slice has overlapping points with its neighbors, which are added together to form the complete convolution.
    
    This process is only possible because of the type of padding used. There are three types of padding commonly used by CNNs. Let $I_d,K_d,O_d$ be the size of each dimension $d$ of the image, kernel, and output arrays. Using \textit{full} padding: $O=I+K-1$; using \textit{same} padding: $O=I=K$; and using \textit{valid} padding: $O=I-K+1$. Only \textit{full} padding preserves the complete information of the convolution of the overlapping points, enabling overlap-add.

    Standardized convolution complexity:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:tp1}
      [I*K] = (I_xI_yI_z)(K_xK_yK_z)~\texttt{MAC-OP}

    \end{equation}
    That is 1 "conv". To complete a layer we gotta do $K_n.C_n$ of those (\# of kernels x channels) which is like a fuckton. So the question is: how many of those can our accelerator make per second? 

    Let's start with a GPU. Say TITAN X PASCAL like~\cite{fan2021high} used. It's got 3584 CUDA cores and runs at 1.53 GHz. We'll compare 1 CUDA core against 1 HECATE core because lolno. We'll argue how many HECATEs you can fit in an FPGA later (spoilers it's like 13).

\section{Limitations and Future Work}\label{sec:lim}
  In the interest of research integrity, this section discloses the limitations of this project, how they were addressed, and how this project can be expanded upon.
  
  Only the parallel-core version of the architecture was fully synthesized and tested on a live board, due to time- and resource-constraints. From it, we extracted the relevant resource utilization metrics -- DSP, LUT, and FF \todo{any others?} -- for each of the processing stages: FFT, CORDIC, and polar multiplication. These values were used to estimate the resource utilization of the fully-pipelined design. Due to the many optimization routines performed by the synthesis algorithm, our calculations undoubtly incur a margin of error. To account for this, the metrics provided in Section~\ref{sec:test} regarding the pipelined design were calculated assuming no further design optimization can be made (i.e., the worst case scenario). All the calculations performed regarding the evaluation of our architecture are described in Section~\ref{ssec:tp}, for transparency.

  Timing closure proved difficult for the parallel-core synthesis, as its size \todo(provide percentiles) makes it more prone to congestion issues. Separate testing was made using smaller designs while maintaining the largest critical path, which is shared by both the parallel and pipelined versions of the architecture. Timing was successfuly closed at 200MHz in these designs, while the complete parallel design was timed at 150 MHz. Further optimizations of the synthesis process will likely enable higher operational frequencies.
