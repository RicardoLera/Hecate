  \IEEEPARstart{I}{mage} processing demands have increased substantially in the last decade, due in part to the success of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in two-dimensional image recognition. Several accelerator architectures have been developed to attend to these demands; a method that has seen wide success in 2D~\cite{mittal2020survey} and 3D~\cite{mittal2021survey} CNNs.

  A variety of optimization approaches have been developed for the task of accelerating CNNs, including: efficient memory access~\cite{han2016cnn,jiang2021optimized,doumet2024h2pipe}; massive parallelization methods~\cite{jouppi2020domain,choquette2021nvidia} often via systolic-array inference~\cite{chua2022systolic}; optimizing floating-point operations~\cite{fan2021high}; quantizing weights and activations~\cite{he2023research,salah2025post}; and addressing data sparcity~\cite{jiang2021optimized,fan2021high}.

  One approach that has also garnered attention is the application of more efficient convolution algorithms. The standard algorithm known as convolution-over-space, as well as the more widely used matrix-multiplication (MMX), have a base assymptotic complexity of $O(N^2)$, scaling very poorly with image and kernel sizes. This is particularly relevant to 3D CNNs, as the processing load of its convolution layers can quickly outscale all other layers in the network~\cite{hegde2018morph}.

  In 2016, Lavin \& Gray~\cite{lavin2016fast} reviewed the relevant convolution algorithms --- which were, at the time, MMX and FFT convolution --- and proposed a new class of algorithms based on the Coppersmith–Winograd series of matrix multiplication methods~\cite{coppersmith1982asymptotic}. MMX Algorithms with lower assymptotic bounds have been discovered~\cite{karstadt2020matrix,schwartz2023pebbling}, however, they incur increasingly complex transforms which severely hinders their applicability. As such, the so-called Winograd class of convolution algorithms has become the standard for hardware-efficient CNN designs~\cite{shen2018towards,barabasz2019winograd,vardhana2025high}.

  FFT convolution was deemed innefficient for modern CNN designs as its upsides only overweight its downsides for convolutions using large kernels~\cite{lavin2016fast}, a method which has lost prevalence to deeper networks that use small (usually 3x3) kernels~\cite{simonyan2014very}. The algorithm is also not, as of yet, easily implemented in high-level designs, due in large part to its reliance on Complex-domain operations.
  
  Since then, new techquines were found to optimize key parts of the algorithm, such as convolution splitting~\cite{chitsaz2020acceleration}, overlap-add~\cite{chen2024fast}, and mixed-radix FFT~\cite{meng2024high}, as well as spectral CNNs~\cite{pratt2017fcnn,liu2021accelerating, ayat2019spectral,watanabe2021image}. It has also has found success in CNNs required the use of large kernels, such as 3D CNNs~\cite{nguyen2016energy, lan2017high, li20243d}.

  There remains, however, the issue of Complex-domain multiplication. Recent FPGA implementations have addressed the issue using parallelization~\cite{li2024split} and pipelining~\cite{fan2021high}. These methods, however, do not directly address the high operation count of Complex multiplication, instead deploying extra hardware resources to overcome it. As such, their hardware-efficiency (measured in throughput per component) remains the same.

  It is possible to reduce the operation count of Complex multiplication by representing its inputs as polar coordinates~\cite{lera2023hardware}. This was first implemented for CNNs in 2024 by Reis et. al~\cite{reis2024phasor} using high-level-synthesis (HLS), achieving promising results. However, HLS, while an important tool for quickly implementing designs, has been frequently critisized in CNN applications regarding resource underutilization~\cite{xu2024flare,jiang2025fpga,antunes2025fpga}, high power consumption~\cite{salman2020comparative} and general performance issues~\cite{faber2022challenges,ahmed2023autohls,lau2024rapidstream}. This is opposed to RTL models, written in hardware-description languages (HDL), where lower level optimizations are available, at the cost of longer development cycles.

    \IEEEpubidadjcol

  We propose HECATE, an FFT-based 3D convolution accelerator using hardware-efficient polar-coordinate multiplication, pipelined FFT and IFFT chains, and optimized for large images using input slicing and overlap-add. We synthesize the architecture using HDL and implement it in a VC709 FPGA board. By validating our implementation using state-of-the-art benchmarks, we demonstrate the viability of FFT convolution in non-spectral 3D CNNs.

\section{Architecture Design}\label{sec:arch}

  FFT convolution (FCONV) achieves lower complexity via the the Convolution Theorem. Let $a,b$ be two discrete signals in $\mathbb{R}^N$. The convolution of $a$ and $b$ can be represented as:
  %
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:theo}
    a * b = \mathcal{F}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}(a) \cdot \mathcal{F}(b)]\,,
  \end{equation}
  %
  where $\cdot$ represents the pointwise product and $\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathcal{F}^{-1}$ represent the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) and its inverse.

  The pointwise product of two signals incurs $O(N)$ multiplications, whereas convoltion-over-space and MMX incur $O(N^2)$. There are two main caveats to this method: the signals must be transformed to-and-from the Complex domain via a DFT; and the pointwise operation must be performed in the Complex domain. We address these issues in Sections \ref{ssec:fft} and \ref{ssec:cmul}, respectivelly.

  We also make use of overlap-add, a decomposition algorithm used to further decrease complexity, and is described in Section~\ref{ssec:alg}. The resulting system can be fully pipelined to increase its throughput, and the methods used for such as described in Section~\ref{ssec:pipe}.

  \subsection{FFT \& IFFT}\label{ssec:fft}
    FFT is a class of algorithms capable of computing an $N$-point DFT in $O(N.{\log}_{2}N)$. The radix-2 FFT~\cite{gauss1866nachlass,cooley1965algorithm} was chosen for this project due to being easily pipelined with few arithmetic components (see Section~\ref{ssec:pipe}).

    \begin{figure}[htb]\centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{fig/fft-0.pdf}
      \caption{Visual representation of an 8-point radix-2 DIT FFT.}\label{fig:fft}
    \end{figure}
    
    An $N$-point radix-2 FFT breaks down the DFT into ${\log}_{2}N$ steps, each consisting of $\frac{N}{2}$ ``butterfly operations'' followed by $N$ twiddle-factor multiplications; as illustrated by Figure~\ref{fig:fft}. Each ``butterfly'' receives a pair $(A,B)$ and outputs $(A+B,A-B)$, where $A$ and $B$ are Complex numbers. Each result is then multiplied by a corresponding root-of-unity --- known as \textit{twiddle-factor} --- with the form:
    %
    \begin{equation}
      \omega_{N}^{k} = e^{\frac{2{\pi}i}{N}k}\,,
    \end{equation}
    %
    where $k \in \mathbb{Z} \cap [0,N-1]$.

    While the input signal is one-dimensional, it is possible to represent an arbritrary number of dimensions using zero-padding. Let $M$ be a three-dimensional signal of size $M_x,M_y,M_z$. Figure~\ref{fig:Ma} represents $M$ given its dimensions all have size 2. To convolve this array with another signal of the same shape, we must first pad each dimension by $2.dim-1$, in order to convey its dimensionaliry and avoid cyclic convolution. Figure~\ref{fig:Mb} represents the padded signal; the green line representing the order of rasterization.

  \subsection{Complex Multiplication}\label{ssec:cmul}
    Naively, Complex-domain multiplication implies 4 Real-domain multiplications. Let $a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{R}$ and $X,Y \in \mathbb{C} \mid X=a+bi,Y=c+di$. As such:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cmul-rect}
      XY = (a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd) + (ad+bc)i\,.
    \end{equation}

    However, X and Y can also be represented in polar coordinates, or more formally $X,Y \in \mathbb{C} \mid X=A\phase{\alpha_1},Y=B\phase{\alpha_2}$, where $A,B$ are the radial distances and $\alpha_1,\alpha_2$ are the respective angular coordinates. As such:
    %  
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cmul-pol}
      XY = A\phase{\alpha_1} \cdot B\phase{\alpha_2} = AB\phase{\alpha_1+\alpha_2}\,.
    \end{equation}

    This representation reduces the total operation count to 1 Real multiplication and 1 Real addition, which are mutually independent and can thus be parallelized.
    
    In order to convert to-and-from these representations, we implement the trigonometric algorithm CORDIC ({\bf{CO}}ordinate {\bf{R}}otation {\bf{DI}}gital {\bf{C}}omputer)~\cite{volder1959cordic}. This algorithm iterativelly calculates the rotation of a given vector by decreasingly large angles. It has two modes of operation: rotation and vectorization; as well as three inputs, here labeled $(x,y,z)$. In vectorization mode, the rectangular coordinates of a Complex number are inputed as $(x_0,y_0)$, and after $j$ iterations, $(x_j,z_j)$ are the radial distance and angular coordinate, respectively. Similarly, in rotation mode, the polar coordinates of a Complex number are inputed at $(x_0,z_0)$ and after $j$ iterations, $(x_j,y_j)$ are the respective rectangular coordinates. This is generally expressed as:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cordic1}
      \begin{bmatrix}
        x_{j+1} \\
        y_{j+1} \\
        z_{j+1}
      \end{bmatrix}
      =
      \begin{bmatrix}
        x_{j} - \sigma_{j}2^{-j}y_{j} \\
        y_{j} + \sigma_{j}2^{-j}x_{j} \\
        z_{j} + \sigma_{j}\arctan(2^{-j})
      \end{bmatrix}\,,
    \end{equation}
    %
    where
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cordic2}
      \sigma_j = 
      \begin{cases}
        sign(y_j);~in~\textbf{vectorization}~mode \\
        -sign(z_j);~in~\textbf{rotation}~mode
      \end{cases}\,.
    \end{equation}

    The precision of the algorithm is equivalent to $2^{-j}$, or 1-bit of precision per iteration. Note that when using fixed-point notation, it is enough to iterate once for each bit after the point. The total number of bits of a variable is herefore denoted $S_t$, and the number of bits after the point is denoted $S_p$. Our implementation defaults to 24 and 16, respectivelly.

    A simple hardware implementation of CORDIC consists of two $S_t$-bit adders, one $S_p$-bit adder, two variable bit-shifters, three registers, and a lookup table for storing $\arctan(2^{-j})$. More efficient hardware implementations \todo{describe and cite}.
    
    The outputs of $x_j$ and $y_j$ are scaled by $K_j$ on each iteration, where
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cordic3}
      K_j = \cos(\arctan(2^{-j}))\,,
    \end{equation}
    and must be corrected to obtain the desired result. The correction can be made after the process completes, by diving each output by the scaling factor:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cordic4}
      \frac{1}{K} = \prod_{j=0}^{S_p-1}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2^{-2j}}}} \stackrel{S_p=16}{\approx} 1.64676\,.
    \end{equation}

    In the pointwise multiplication step, CORDIC is used 3 times. The first two convert the input image slice and kernel to polar coordinates, and the last converts the result back to rectangular coordinates. The scaling factor carries over across operations, including pointwise multiplication, and as such we can apply the correction at any point in the process (image, kernel or product). The combined scaling factor is:
    %
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:cordic5}
      \frac{1}{K^3} \stackrel{S_p=16}{\approx} 0.22393\,.
    \end{equation}

    The values of $\arctan(2^{-j})$ are also constant and can be pre-calculated and stored in a lookup table. However, the convolution operation requires adding two angular coordinates before the transformation back into rectangular coordinates. The resulting angle can exceed $\pi$ radians, causing issues with the algorithm. To circumvent this, we store the \textit{quadrant} of each angular coordinate in the unit circle, normalize them to the first quadrant, perform the add operation, and convert the result into the respective quadrant. To simplify this computation, we divide each $\arctan(2^{-j})$ by $2\pi$. The resulting binary, encoded in 16-bits, represents fractions of $\pi$, with its two most-significant bits representing the quadrant of the vector in the unit circle, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pfb}. This allows quick access to the quadrant of the angle, as well as enabling normalization to any quadrant by simply setting its first two bits.

    \begin{figure}[htb]\centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{fig/unit_circle-0.pdf}
      \caption{Four symmetric angles encoded as $\pi$-fractions.}\label{fig:pfb}
    \end{figure}
    
    CORDIC's complexity does not increase with image- or kernel-size, depending instead on $S_p$, giving it a relative asymptotic complexity of $O(1)$ and enabling a trade-off between numerical precision and operation complexity.

    CORDIC can also been applied to FFTs to more efficiently calculate twiddle-factor multiplications~\cite{banerjee2001fpga,choudhary2011cordic}. Each CORDIC iteration rotates the input by elementary angles which coincide with the twiddle-factors used by the FFT. This, however, also requires scaling each point that wasn't multiplied by the twiddle-factor, to account for the gain induced by CORDIC, resuling in an increase of LUT components used. As later described in Section~\ref{sec:imp}, LUTs are the limiting factor in scaling this design, not DSPs. As such, we elected to implement the twiddle-factor rotations using DSP blocks.
    
  \subsection{Algorithmic Decomposition}\label{ssec:alg}
    In order to convolve two inputs using FCONV, the size of the input arrays must be equivalent. However, in the field of image recognition, the image is often much larger than the convolution kernels. In order to avoid padding the kernel to match the image, we employ a decomposition algorithm known as overlap-add~\cite{smith1997scientist,chen2024fast}. The image is broken down into kernel-sized slices which are individually convolved with each kernel. Each slice has overlapping points with its neighbors, which are added together to form the complete convolution.
    
    This process is only possible because of the type of padding used. There are three types of padding commonly used by CNNs. Let $I_d,K_d,O_d$ be the size of each dimension $d$ of the image, kernel, and output arrays. Using \textit{full} padding: $O=I+K-1$; using \textit{same} padding: $O=I=K$; and using \textit{valid} padding: $O=I-K+1$. Only \textit{full} padding preserves the complete information of the convolution of the overlapping points, enabling overlap-add.

In the interest of fair comparison, we will standardize these parameters, sourcing the original technical reports for C3D and UCF101.
