  \IEEEPARstart{I}{mage} processing demands have increased substantially in the last decade, due in part to the success of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in two-dimensional image recognition. The step into the third dimension poses a difficult task for CNN architectures, as the complexity of the convolution layers outscale every other processing layer, including Fully Connected layers, and often consuming over 99\% of total processing time~\cite{hegde2018morph}. To mitigate this issue, several targetted accelerator architectures have been developed, a method that has seen wide success in two-dimensional convolution~\cite{mittal2020survey}.

  A consequence of large-scale image processing acceleration is a steep increase in power requirements, as seen in commercial 2D accelerators such as the TPU~\cite{jouppi2020domain} and Tensor Cores~\cite{choquette2021nvidia}. This is due to the parallelization techniques employed by these applications, such as systolic array, which incur extra arithmetic blocks. These techniques do not reduce the overall complexity of the algorithm (i.e., the total number of elemental operations required to complete the task) instead employing extra hardware proportional to the desired speed gain. % In order to reduce power consumption while maintaining performance, %% I'll mention this later

  A 2021 survey~\cite{mittal2021survey} of accelerator architectures for 3D CNNs across multiple platforms highlights the particular challenges of acceleration in 3D space, and how 2D accelerators cannot be naivelly applied to a higher dimensional space while maintaining efficiency. This further emphasises the need for acceleration techniques beyond parallelism, as the required hardware for this task grows in one extra dimension.

  FPGA and ASIC implementations generally display higher power-efficiency, with the former being more prevalent in the field due to its ease of implementation. While ASICs can be designed to be even more efficient, their design and implementation costs often outweight their benefits~\cite{mittal2021survey}. When employing these platforms, an additional metric is used to measure the efficiency of an accelerator: \textit{hardware-efficiency}, defined as the ratio between the performance gain and hardware cost. In FPGA, performance is generally measured in GOPS (giga-operations per second), and hardware cost is often used interchangeably with  DSP consumption, as these are the blocks capable of processing complex arithmetic tasks most efficiently. Additionaly, the metric is often divided by the clock frequency used, to better evaluate the architecture's design regardless of the frequency limitations of its platform.

  We reviewed recent developments in the field concerning 3D convolution accelerators in FPGA. This body of work revealed a tendency to employ more optimal convolution algorithms, while addressing their particular needs.   \subsection{Numeric Representation}
    Our design uses fixed-point representation. The bit size and point location are arbitrary and chosen during synthesis to enable a trade-off between performance and precision.

    The choice between fixed-point and floating-point was not trivial. Fixed-point can represent values in the range $2^{{\pm}n}$, where $n$ is the number of bits; while floating-point representations disperse their values in a wider exponential range, increasing precision for values closer to zero. Recent FPGA designs have also included floating-point arithmetic cells, bringing performance closer to that of a fixed-point application.

      (a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd) + (ad+bc)i
    \end{equation}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:rela}
  Fan et al.~\cite{fan2021high} presents an accelerator which processes C3D in 93.95 ms using 1345 DSPs and a frequency of 220 MHz, giving it a hardware-efficiency of $5.19 [\frac{GOP}{s{\cdot}MHz{\cdot}DSP}]$. Their method involves quantization of weights and activations with static block floating point, while the convolution algorithm remains the standard matrix-multiplication method. This method, however, does not reduce the overall number of multiplication operations required by the convolution layer, which are still performed using fixed-point representation.
